KF-L001-B02 · The Evolution of Knowledge Engineering

The Expanding Object of Engineering from Expert Systems to Human–AI Knowledge Systems

Evidence · Final · v1.0 · 40 min read

Question  →  Corpus  →  Evidence  →  Patterns  →  Candidate Insight

Purpose

To establish the historical and methodological evolution of Knowledge Engineering and to determine, from the literature, whether the field’s trajectory points toward a genuine extension or a recombination of existing capabilities. The chapter reads the field’s history through the changing object of engineering, accounts explicitly for the traditions that have treated knowledge change as an engineering problem, and defines — as a falsifiable burden for any future methodology — the candidate gap of cross-layer governance of knowledge change: the controlled transition of a knowledge system from one justified state to another.

Executive Summary

Knowledge Engineering has evolved continuously since the expert-systems era of the 1970s. While its technologies, methods and applications have changed substantially, the field exhibits a consistent structural pattern: the object being engineered has progressively expanded — from expert reasoning, to formal representations, to knowledge-intensive systems, to shared conceptual models, to connected knowledge ecosystems, and most recently to hybrid systems in which knowledge is distributed across human expertise, explicit stores, and the parameters of large models.

This document argues that the history of Knowledge Engineering is best understood not as a succession of technologies, but as a progression in what practitioners sought to engineer. This lens allows each period to be assessed by the engineering problem it addressed, the capability it contributed, and the limitation that motivated its successor.

Two corrections distinguish this version from earlier drafts. First, knowledge change has not been absent from the field’s history: truth maintenance, belief revision, ontology evolution, schema and database evolution, provenance, and — most recently — model editing have each treated revision, temporal state and controlled modification as engineering problems. Any claim of novelty for a new methodology must account for these traditions. Second, and consequently, the candidate contribution is not “engineering knowledge change” — which already exists — but the cross-layer governance of change: an integrating methodology for how modifications to knowledge are proposed, evidenced, challenged, authorised, propagated, evaluated and released across an entire human–machine knowledge system. Stated more precisely, the candidate object is the controlled transition of a knowledge system from one justified state to another.

Whether such an integrating methodology constitutes a meaningful extension of Knowledge Engineering, or a recombination of mature but separately developed parts, remains an empirical question reserved for the Synthesis (C01) and the methodology work that follows it. This chapter places no methodology inside the historical record; it defines the burden of proof that any such methodology must discharge.

Orientation for the Standalone Reader

This is the first evidence chapter of the Literature Landscape (chapter order: B02 → B03 → B04 → B05 → B01 → C01) and is intended to be read on its own. It examines the engineering tradition that builds knowledge systems — knowledge held explicitly in rules, ontologies and graphs, and knowledge held implicitly in the parameters of machine-learning models — and reads that history through the changing object of engineering. Where later chapters presuppose terms such as “justified state,” “layer” or “cross-layer governance of change,” those terms are introduced here. A reader beginning with B02 loses nothing; a reader arriving from a later chapter will find the historical foundation on which the others build.

1. Introduction

The purpose of this document is not to provide another historical survey of Knowledge Engineering. Detailed histories of artificial intelligence, expert systems and knowledge-based systems already exist (Buchanan & Shortliffe 1984; Studer, Benjamins & Fensel 1998; Schreiber et al. 2000). Instead, this document asks a more diagnostic question:

What was the object being engineered at each stage in the evolution of Knowledge Engineering?

This question is revealing because mature engineering disciplines evolve by progressively expanding the systems they seek to design, manage and optimise. Mechanical engineering expanded from individual machines to integrated manufacturing systems. Software engineering expanded from programs to distributed and socio-technical systems (Naur & Randell 1969; Sommerville 2016). Systems engineering expanded from discrete products to systems-of-systems. This chapter investigates whether Knowledge Engineering exhibits a comparable trajectory, and, if so, what candidate object that trajectory reveals for future methodological work.

A note on method and restraint. The account below examines each period on its own terms before any inference is drawn about a candidate next stage, and it deliberately treats the contemporary AI period as a convergence of traditions rather than as the uninterrupted continuation of a single lineage. The risk this chapter is written to avoid is the retrospective fallacy — reading the whole history as though it were always converging on a preferred conclusion. The corrective is the six-dimension analytical lens in Section 3, which forces each period to be described by the problem it faced rather than by its relationship to a predetermined conclusion, together with the explicit treatment of knowledge-change traditions in Section 12, which tests the central claim against the literatures most likely to disconfirm it.

2. Central Research Question

The chapter is organised around one guiding question:

How has the object of engineering changed throughout the history of Knowledge Engineering?

Supporting questions include: What engineering problem did each generation attempt to solve? What assumptions did it inherit? What limitations motivated its successor? Which problems recur across the whole history? And does the contemporary AI period fundamentally alter the engineering problem, or re-instantiate older problems in new form?

3. Analytical Lens

Rather than organising the literature by publication date, by technology, or by the reputations of individual researchers, this chapter analyses each period according to six dimensions.

3.1 A Note on Terminology: Maintenance, Revision, Evolution and Governance

Because this chapter’s central argument concerns knowledge change, and because the surrounding literature uses the relevant terms loosely, the following working distinctions are adopted throughout. They prevent “governed evolution” from operating as an attractive but underspecified phrase.

Term Working meaning in this chapter
Maintenance Activities that keep a knowledge artefact usable (correction, upkeep, error repair).
Update Addition or replacement of content.
Revision Modification in response to changed evidence, interpretation or requirements.
Evolution Cumulative change in content, structure, relationships, authority or use over time.
Lifecycle The stages through which a knowledge artefact or system passes.
Governance The decision rights, rules and accountability applied across those processes — who may change what, on what evidence, with what authority, and to what released state.

The distinction that carries the most analytical weight is between representing that a change occurred (which several existing traditions do well) and governing whether a change is justified, authorised and fit to be promoted into an accepted knowledge state (which is less consolidated). Section 12 develops this distinction; Sections 13–16 build the candidate gap upon it.

4. The Expanding Object of Engineering

The central analytical proposition is that Knowledge Engineering has progressively expanded its object of engineering. The table below states the established historical sequence as a working hypothesis; each row is examined against the literature in Sections 6–12 before any claim of progression is accepted. Consistent with the review, the historical sequence now ends at the contemporary convergence; no candidate methodology is placed as a further row, so that any later positioning is argued rather than presupposed.

Period / Era Approximate Primary Engineering Object Representative Contributions
Knowledge Acquisition 1970s–1980s Expert knowledge Feigenbaum; Buchanan; DENDRAL; MYCIN
Knowledge Representation 1980s Rules, frames, semantic networks, logic Newell; Minsky; Brachman
Knowledge Modelling 1990s Knowledge-intensive systems KADS; CommonKADS; MIKE
Knowledge Organisation 1990s–2000s Ontologies and shared conceptual models Gruber; Guarino
Semantic Interoperability 2000s Machine-readable distributed knowledge Semantic Web (Berners-Lee et al.)
Connected Knowledge 2010s Knowledge graphs and linked ecosystems Linked Data; enterprise knowledge graphs
Contemporary Convergence 2020s Hybrid Symbolic–Parametric Knowledge Systems Foundation models, RAG, tools and agents

Candidate integrative object under investigation: the governed evolution of hybrid human–machine knowledge systems — examined, but not asserted, in Sections 12–16 and reserved for evaluation beyond the Landscape.

5. Method of Analysis

Each period is analysed using a consistent internal structure: Historical Context; Engineering Object; Core Methods; Major Contributions; Remaining Limitations; Transition; and Relevance to Governed Knowledge Evolution.

6. Engineering Expert Reasoning (1970s–1980s)

Historical context. Knowledge Engineering emerged from a specific empirical discovery of 1960s–1970s artificial intelligence: that competent performance on hard, real-world problems depended less on general-purpose reasoning than on large quantities of task-specific knowledge. The DENDRAL project at Stanford, initiated by Lederberg, Feigenbaum and Buchanan, showed that a program could rival specialist chemists at inferring molecular structure from mass-spectrometry data only when domain heuristics were painstakingly encoded (Lindsay et al. 1980; Buchanan & Feigenbaum 1978). MYCIN followed, applying roughly six hundred production rules to the diagnosis of bacterial infections, and introduced certainty factors for reasoning under uncertainty and an explanation facility able to justify its conclusions to clinicians (Shortliffe 1976; Buchanan & Shortliffe 1984). The subsequent abstraction of MYCIN’s inference engine into the reusable shell EMYCIN marked the first clear separation of reasoning machinery from domain knowledge (van Melle 1979).

Engineering object. The object of engineering was expert reasoning itself — the heuristic, often tacit knowledge distinguishing an expert from a competent novice. Feigenbaum’s “knowledge principle” crystallised the era’s central claim: that an intelligent system’s power resides primarily in the knowledge it possesses, and only secondarily in its inference procedures (Feigenbaum 1977).

Core methods. The dominant methods were knowledge acquisition through structured interviews with experts, and representation as production rules of the form condition → action. The prevailing epistemology was a transfer view: expert knowledge was assumed to exist in finished form inside the expert’s head, from which the knowledge engineer’s task was to extract and transcribe it.

Major contributions. This era established Knowledge Engineering as a named activity, demonstrated that narrow but genuinely expert-level performance was achievable, and produced two durable ideas: the separation of knowledge base from inference engine, and the requirement that systems explain their conclusions — an early and prescient concern with transparency.

Remaining limitations. Three limitations proved decisive. First, the knowledge acquisition bottleneck: eliciting and encoding expert knowledge was slow, costly and error-prone, and became the recognised rate-limiting step (Feigenbaum 1977; Hayes-Roth, Waterman & Lenat 1983). Second, brittleness: systems performed well within their narrow envelope but failed gracelessly at its edges, lacking any model of what they did not know (McCarthy 1983). Third, maintenance and scale: large, flat rule bases became difficult to modify without unintended interaction, and the knowledge they held was static.

Transition. The acquisition bottleneck and brittleness together motivated more principled ways to represent knowledge and, later, more disciplined ways to model whole systems.

Relevance to governed knowledge evolution. The knowledge/inference separation and the explanation imperative are foundational commitments any governed knowledge systemGoverned knowledge system — a socio-technical system that holds operational knowledge objects and governs their transition from one justified state to another across modalities (KF-1001). Vocabulary → should preserve. But the era’s two failures — the acquisition bottleneck and static brittleness — are precisely the problems a claim about continuous, evolving knowledge must address rather than reproduce. The acquisition bottleneck, in particular, never disappears; it recurs in later periods as ontology-population cost, graph-curation cost and, in the AI period, as the grounding and freshness problem.

7. Engineering Formal Knowledge (1980s)

Historical context. As the limits of ad-hoc rule bases became apparent, attention turned to the formal structures in which knowledge could be represented independently of any single expert or application.

Engineering object. The engineering object expanded from one expert’s reasoning to formal knowledge structures — representations designed to be inspectable, general and, ideally, reusable across problems.

Core methods. Four traditions matured in parallel. Semantic networks modelled knowledge as labelled graphs of concepts and relations (Quillian 1968). Frames organised knowledge into structured, default-bearing stereotypes of situations and objects, capturing the expectation-driven character of human understanding (Minsky 1974). Logic-based representation pursued formal expressiveness and sound inference, giving rise to the structured inheritance networks and terminological reasoning of the KL-ONE family — the direct ancestor of the description logics later underpinning the Semantic Web (Brachman & Schmolze 1985). Cutting across these was Newell’s Knowledge Level: the proposal that a system can be described at a level of abstraction above its symbolic implementation, at which it is characterised purely by the knowledge it has and the rationality principle that it acts to achieve its goals given that knowledge (Newell 1982).

Major contributions. The era’s lasting contribution was as much conceptual as technical. Newell’s Knowledge Level gave the field a vocabulary for discussing knowledge independently of how it is implemented — a distinction on which all later modelling, ontology and interoperability work depends. The representation formalisms themselves became mature engineering primitives, and the expressiveness–tractability trade-off was made explicit (Brachman & Levesque 1984): richer representations permit more to be said but make sound inference computationally harder.

Remaining limitations. The era advanced how to represent knowledge but said little about how to build knowledge-based systems systematically. Two engineers given the same formalism could still produce incompatible, unmaintainable systems. There was, as yet, no engineering methodology governing the construction, validation and reuse of knowledge structures at the level of a whole system.

Transition. The absence of methodology drove the next period.

Relevance to governed knowledge evolution. The Knowledge Level is the single most important inheritance for framing governed knowledge evolution, because such a claim is most coherently stated at the knowledge level rather than at the level of any particular store, model or format. Representational pluralism is a second inheritance: no single formalism dominates, and a modern knowledge system must expect to govern knowledge that is simultaneously symbolic (graphs, ontologies) and sub-symbolic (model parameters).

8. Engineering Knowledge-Intensive Systems (1990s)

Historical context. During the 1990s the field’s centre of gravity shifted from representation to method. The animating recognition — associated above all with the KADS and CommonKADS programmes and with Clancey’s re-analysis of expert systems — was that building a knowledge-based system is not an act of mining pre-existing knowledge but an act of constructing a model of problem-solving behaviour (Clancey 1985; Wielinga, Schreiber & Breuker 1992; Schreiber et al. 2000).

Engineering object. The engineering object expanded again — from isolated knowledge structures to knowledge-intensive systems as engineered artefacts, and to the engineering process itself.

Core methods. CommonKADS supplied a suite of interlocking models rather than a single representation: organisation, task, agent, communication, knowledge and design models, spanning the analysis of why a system is built through to how it is realised (Schreiber et al. 2000). A parallel current formalised reusable problem-solving methods (PSMs) — abstract, domain-independent inference patterns such as heuristic classification, cover-and-differentiate or propose-and-revise — that could be selected and configured for new tasks (Clancey 1985; Chandrasekaran 1986; McDermott 1988; Marcus & McDermott 1989). The MIKE approach integrated formal specification with a model-based, incremental development cycle (Angele, Fensel & Studer 1998).

Major contributions. Three contributions endure. First, the decisive epistemological shift from the transfer view to the modelling view: knowledge engineering came to be understood as the construction of a justified model, not the extraction of a pre-existing truth — a shift with direct implications for provenance and revisability. Second, the explicit incorporation of organisational context. Third, the ideal of reuse through libraries of task templates and problem-solving methods.

Remaining limitations. The methodologies were heavyweight and oriented toward large bespoke enterprise systems. Reuse remained largely aspirational because there was, as yet, no widely shared substrate of reusable domain content — the reusable components were largely inferential (PSMs), not conceptual. Interoperability between independently built systems was essentially unaddressed.

Transition. The unmet need for reusable, shareable domain conceptualisations — as opposed to reusable inference patterns — drove the field directly into ontology engineering.

Relevance to governed knowledge evolution. This is arguably the closest historical antecedent to the ambitions of governed knowledge evolution. It is also the point at which Knowledge Engineering acquired characteristics associated with systems engineering: explicit lifecycle methods, multiple coordinated models, organisational analysis, and traceability from requirements to design. (This is an interpretive observation about shared characteristics, not a claim that Knowledge Engineering was formally subsumed within systems engineering as a discipline.) The model-suite architecture, the lifecycle orientation and above all the modelling stance — knowledge as constructed and therefore accountable to its justification — are the intellectual raw material of any “governed evolution” claim. The limitation to guard against is heavyweightness: a system claiming to govern continuous evolution cannot inherit a method designed for one-off, up-front construction.

9. Engineering Shared Meaning (1990s–2000s)

Historical context. The reuse problem left open by the methodology period found its answer in the ontology: an explicit, shared specification of the concepts and relations of a domain, against which multiple systems could align.

Engineering object. The engineering object expanded from individual systems to shared conceptual models — meaning designed to be common across people, systems and organisations.

Core methods. The most widely cited formulation associated with Gruber’s work defines an ontology as “an explicit specification of a conceptualization” (Gruber 1993, 1995). A distinct and stronger formulation is offered by Studer, Benjamins and Fensel, for whom an ontology is “a formal, explicit specification of a shared conceptualization” — a phrasing that names all four engineering commitments (formality, explicitness, sharing and conceptual abstraction) in a single clause (Studer, Benjamins & Fensel 1998). These are two related but non-identical definitions rather than variants of one original sentence; the Studer formulation, by making shared explicit, does most of the work relevant to this chapter. Guarino brought formal-ontological rigour to the enterprise, distinguishing ontological levels and insisting on principled distinctions rather than ad-hoc taxonomies (Guarino 1998). This body of work was given a web-scale ambition by the Semantic Web vision, which proposed a layered stack — RDF for assertions, RDFS and OWL for schema and ontology — through which machine-readable meaning could be published and interlinked across the open web (Berners-Lee, Hendler & Lassila 2001).

Major contributions. The period produced the field’s first genuinely shareable knowledge substrate and a standardised technology stack for it, and reframed interoperability as a problem of shared meaning rather than shared format.

Remaining limitations. Adoption was the recurring difficulty. Formal ontology engineering was labour-intensive and demanded scarce expertise; the acquisition bottleneck reappeared as an ontology-construction bottleneck. The most expressive, philosophically rigorous ontologies were often the least widely adopted, while practical uptake favoured lightweight vocabularies.

Transition. The gap between heavyweight formal ontologies and practical adoption pushed the field toward a pragmatic, data-first posture — the Linked Data turn.

Relevance to governed knowledge evolution. The “shared conceptualisation” is a load-bearing inheritance: governed evolution presupposes some shared conceptual frame against which change can be defined and validated. The cautionary inheritance is the adoption problem — formal rigour that is not adopted governs nothing.

10. Engineering Connected Knowledge Ecosystems (2010s)

Historical context. The 2010s moved the emphasis from formally specifying meaning to connecting knowledge at scale. The Linked Data principles reframed the Semantic Web as a pragmatic practice of publishing and interlinking dereferenceable data (Berners-Lee 2006; Bizer, Heath & Berners-Lee 2009), giving rise to large open datasets such as DBpedia and, later, to the wide industrial adoption of knowledge graphs — a term popularised when Google applied it to its own large-scale entity graph in 2012 (Singhal 2012; Hogan et al. 2021).

Engineering object. The engineering object expanded from shared conceptual models to connected knowledge ecosystems — large, heterogeneous, continuously integrated graphs of entities and relations spanning many sources and domains.

Core methods. The graph became the integration substrate. Methods combined ontology-based schemas with statistical and machine-learning techniques for population, entity resolution, link prediction and quality assessment. Enterprise knowledge graphs applied the same substrate inside organisations, unifying siloed data around a shared entity model (Noy et al. 2019; Hogan et al. 2021). Definitional debate accompanied adoption, as the field worked to state precisely what distinguishes a knowledge graph from a database or an ontology (Ehrlinger & Wöß 2016).

Major contributions. The period demonstrated that knowledge could be engineered as a large, evolving, interconnected ecosystem rather than a bounded artefact, serving as durable infrastructure across many applications. Provenance and identity — which source asserted what, and which real-world entity a node denotes — became first-class engineering concerns at scale (Moreau & Missier 2013).

Remaining limitations. Two limitations are pivotal for this chapter. First, evolution and maintenance: knowledge graphs are expensive to keep current, complete and consistent, and in practice many are curated snapshots that decay. Second, quality and provenance over time: as graphs are continuously updated from heterogeneous sources, tracking justification, currency and trust across successive states is only partially handled. The precise maturity of these capabilities is not a conclusion that can be drawn from a general knowledge-graph survey; it should be assessed through the dedicated ontology-evolution and temporal-knowledge-graph literatures (see Section 12).

Transition. The cost of populating and maintaining graphs pushed the field toward machine learning and ultimately toward foundation models, while sharpening the recognition that connected knowledge must be continuously maintained, not merely once connected.

Relevance to governed knowledge evolution. The ecosystem framing, and the elevation of provenance and identity to first-class concerns, are strong inheritances. This is also the period in which the temporal problem — governing knowledge as it changes rather than as it stands — becomes clearly visible as an implementation reality, even if not yet named as the field’s central object.

11. Engineering Hybrid Symbolic–Parametric Systems (2020s)

The Contemporary Convergence: Knowledge Engineering Meets Foundation Models

Historical context and a caution about lineage. Unlike the preceding periods, the contemporary AI period did not straightforwardly emerge as the next stage of the Knowledge Engineering tradition. Foundation models and agentic systems arose largely through machine learning and natural-language processing, historically often in tension with symbolic Knowledge Engineering rather than in continuity with it (Bommasani et al. 2021). It is therefore treated here as a convergence of previously distinct traditions, not as an uninterrupted lineage.

Engineering object. The distinctive object of this period is the Hybrid Symbolic–Parametric Knowledge System, in which knowledge is distributed across: human expertise; explicit stores (documents, databases); graphs and ontologies; model parameters; retrieval processes; external tools; and interaction histories. This distributed object is analytically sharper than the broad category “human–AI knowledge systems,” which would also include interface design, work redesign and decision support that are not primarily about the location and movement of knowledge.

Core methods. Several methods define the period. Retrieval-Augmented Generation combines parametric generation with access to an external, updateable corpus (Lewis et al. 2020). Agentic systems wrap models in planning, tool-use and memory so that they can decompose goals and act over multiple steps (Yao et al. 2023; Schick et al. 2023). Machine memory research pursues persistent, updatable context across interactions. Model editing provides targeted modification of specific factual associations within a model’s parameters (Meng et al. 2022, 2023). Human–AI collaboration and neuro-symbolic integration study the co-production and mutual correction of knowledge.

Major contributions. The period has delivered unprecedented breadth and fluency of access to encoded knowledge and, through retrieval and tool use, a practical architecture for combining the coverage of parametric models with the accountability of explicit stores. Foundation models changed the representational distribution of knowledge within intelligent systems, because substantial factual and procedural capability is now encoded implicitly in model parameters rather than solely in explicit, inspectable stores. (This is a claim about where knowledge sits and how it is accessed; it does not require the stronger, philosophically loaded claim that the ontological status of knowledge has changed.)

Remaining limitations. The period’s limitations map with striking precision onto the unresolved problems of earlier periods. Hallucination is the modern face of brittleness (Section 6): confident failure outside a reliable envelope. Staleness and grounding are the parametric form of the currency problem (Section 10). Retrieval-Augmented Generation can improve currency and create the possibility of source traceability, although reliable provenance and citation require additional system design beyond retrieval itself. Opacity weakens the explanation imperative of Section 6: parametric knowledge is difficult to inspect, attribute or selectively revise. And governance of what such systems know, and how that changes over time, remains difficult. Parametric knowledge can be updated through retraining, fine-tuning or targeted model-editing methods, and model artefacts can plainly be versioned and released; what remains difficult is selective revision with reliable locality, semantic consistency, traceable justification and predictable downstream consequences comparable to those available in explicit knowledge stores — as the model-editing literature itself documents in its continuing struggles with locality and scaling (Meng et al. 2022, 2023).

Automatic acquisition — a precise statement. Foundation models have dramatically reduced the marginal cost of extracting, reformulating and generating candidate knowledge at scale, without eliminating the need for validation, grounding, reconciliation and structured integration. This is the sense in which the acquisition bottleneck of Section 6 has been eased but not removed.

Transition / candidate. It is precisely this cluster — evolution, provenance, quality-over-time, governance, and the co-production of hybrid symbolic-and-parametric knowledge — that motivates the candidate integrative object. The convergence supplies the capabilities but not, thus far, the governing discipline that spans them.

Relevance to governed knowledge evolution. This period is the immediate context and foil for any governed knowledge methodology. The inheritances are the hybrid architecture (retrieval + generation + agency) and the practical easing of acquisition. The problems such a methodology must be shown to address — rather than restate — are the cross-layer governance, provenance and evolution of knowledge that is now partly opaque and partly parametric.

12. Traditions of Knowledge Revision and Evolution (The Overlooked Evolution Traditions)

Before concluding anything about a gap, the chapter must account for the literatures most directly concerned with knowledge change. A knowledgeable reviewer could otherwise object, correctly, that a gap has been “found” only because the traditions that treated change as an engineering problem were excluded from the history. This section asks: which prior traditions already treated change, revision and temporal consistency as engineering problems, even if they did not integrate them into a full socio-technical methodology?

Truth maintenance. Truth-maintenance systems introduced machinery for tracking the justifications of beliefs and for retracting and restoring conclusions as premises change, so that a reasoning system’s state could be kept consistent under revision (Doyle 1979). Change was, here, an explicit engineering concern at the level of dependency-directed belief.

Belief revision. The AGM framework formalised how a rational agent should contract and revise a logically closed body of belief when confronted with new or conflicting information, subject to postulates such as minimal change and consistency (Alchourrón, Gärdenfors & Makinson 1985; Gärdenfors 1988). This is arguably the most rigorous formal treatment of knowledge change in the entire history of the field.

Ontology change and evolution. The Semantic Web community developed a dedicated body of work on ontology evolution — distinguished carefully from database schema evolution — addressing how large, collaboratively developed conceptual structures should be modified, versioned and kept compatible over time (Noy & Klein 2004; Flouris et al. 2008). Notably, this tradition explicitly connected itself to belief revision, proposing that AGM-style theory change be adapted to govern ontology modification (Flouris et al. 2008) — evidence that these “separate” traditions were already aware of one another.

Database, schema and temporal-data evolution. Long-standing work in databases addressed schema versioning and evolution (Roddick 1995) and the representation of time-varying data in temporal databases (Snodgrass 1999); the graph-era descendant of this concern is the temporal knowledge graph, which represents facts whose validity is time-bounded and reasons over changing graph states (e.g. Trivedi et al. 2017).

Provenance. Provenance research provides formal machinery for recording how a piece of data or knowledge was produced, influenced and derived. Crucially, the W3C PROV data model represents revision itself as a type of derivation between entities (Moreau & Missier 2013), and workflow-provenance research does the same for scientific processes (Davidson & Freire 2008). This means the chapter must not claim that formal knowledge revision is absent from the record.

Configuration and version management. Software configuration management developed mature models for versioning, branching, merging and releasing changing artefacts under controlled processes (Conradi & Westfechtel 1998) — a body of engineering practice about governed change that predates most of the knowledge-specific work above.

What these traditions establish, and what they leave open. Taken together, these literatures show that change, revision, temporal state and controlled modification have repeatedly been treated as first-class engineering problems. Each, however, addresses change within a particular representational, technical or organisational layer: beliefs, an ontology, a schema, a graph, a provenance record, a software artefact, a model’s parameters. Moreover — and this is the sharper point — a system that can represent that a revision occurred does not thereby determine whether that revision is epistemically justified, organisationally authorised, or suitable for promotion into an accepted knowledge state. PROV can record that entity B was a revision of entity A; it does not, and does not attempt to, adjudicate whether B should replace A across the systems and people that depend on it.

That distinction reframes the entire enquiry. The candidate contribution is not knowledge change itself, which demonstrably exists across many layers. It is the cross-layer governance of change: an integrating methodology for how modifications are proposed, evidenced, challenged, authorised, propagated, evaluated and released across an entire Hybrid Symbolic–Parametric Knowledge System.

13. Comparative Analysis

Rather than comparing technologies, this section compares engineering objectives across the history.

Which problems have matured. Representation formalisms are mature and well understood (Section 7). Ontology languages are standardised (Section 9). Large-scale integration via graphs is an established industrial practice (Section 10). Access to large bodies of encoded knowledge has advanced dramatically (Section 11).

Which problems recur. Two problems recur in every period under changing names. The acquisition problem appears as the elicitation bottleneck (Section 6), the ontology-construction bottleneck (Section 9) and the graph-curation cost (Section 10), before being eased — not eliminated — by automatic extraction (Section 11). The reliability-at-the-boundary problem appears as brittleness (Section 6) and re-emerges as hallucination (Section 11). Their persistence suggests they are structural features of engineering knowledge, not accidents of a given technology.

Which assumptions persist. Three assumptions survive the whole history: that knowledge is a legitimate object of engineering; that explicit structure has value, even as parametric methods rise, because structure is what makes knowledge inspectable, shareable and governable; and that knowledge systems are socio-technical, with the human context part of the system rather than external to it.

The recast central finding. The strongest formulation the evidence supports is the following.

Across the history reviewed here, knowledge change has not been absent as an engineering concern. It has appeared in knowledge-base maintenance, truth maintenance, belief revision, organisational learning, ontology evolution, provenance, graph updating and model adaptation. These traditions, however, usually address change within a particular representational, technical or organisational layer. What appears less consolidated is a cross-layer methodology governing how changes to knowledge are proposed, evidenced, challenged, authorised, propagated, evaluated and released across a Hybrid Symbolic–Parametric Knowledge System. This is the narrower and more defensible candidate gap against which any future methodology should be evaluated.

This claim is analytically much harder to refute than the earlier “change was never central,” because it concedes that change was central within layers and locates the candidate contribution in their integration.

13.1 Provisional Cross-Stage Patterns

The following patterns are offered as provisional cross-stage patterns supported by the present review, not as validated findings; a narrative synthesis of selected canonical sources cannot establish more than that.

Crucially, the field’s engineering capabilities did not replace one another in a clean succession. Acquisition remains central today; representation runs throughout; forms of governance existed in earlier layers. The more accurate picture is accretive:

Acquisition + Representation + Modelling + Sharing + Interoperability
            + Temporal maintenance + Governance

Figure B02·2 — the field’s capabilities accrete rather than replace one another.

The field accumulated engineering capabilities rather than moving cleanly from one function to the next. The object progression (expert reasoning → formal representation → knowledge-intensive systems → semantic ecosystems → connected ecosystems → hybrid symbolic–parametric systems) is likewise a widening of scope rather than a replacement sequence, and — consistent with Section 4 — it ends at the contemporary convergence, with governed evolution positioned as a candidate integrative object rather than an established further stage.

13.2 Limitations of This Review

This chapter is a work of interpretive synthesis, and its conclusions should be read with four limitations in mind.

First, it is a selective narrative synthesis rather than a systematic review. Sources were chosen for their canonical standing and their bearing on the “object of engineering” argument, not assembled through an exhaustive, protocol-driven search strategy. The absence of a given tradition or source here is therefore not evidence of its absence from the field.

Second, it is organised around a single interpretive analytical lens — the changing object of engineering. This lens foregrounds certain developments and necessarily backgrounds others; a different organising principle (for example, one centred on applications, institutions, economics or user communities) would yield a different, and potentially equally legitimate, history.

Third, it is weighted toward English-language and computational Knowledge Engineering literature. It under-represents scholarship published in other languages and in adjacent research communities, which may have treated some of the same problems — including knowledge change and governance — under different vocabularies.

Fourth, it is not yet a complete treatment of Knowledge Management, archival science, epistemology or organisational governance. These disciplines bear directly on the governance half of the candidate gap identified in Sections 12–16, and the Position Paper will need to engage them more fully than this evidence chapter does; the treatment here should be read as establishing the engineering lineage, not the full interdisciplinary foundation.

These limitations bound the chapter’s claims without undermining its argument: what is offered is a defensible reading of the field’s trajectory and of the candidate gap, not a settled, exhaustive or discipline-complete account.

14. Existing Contributions of Knowledge Engineering

A central objective of this chapter is to state plainly what modern Knowledge Engineering already provides, so that the novelty of any future methodology is neither overstated nor overlooked. The inherited capability base includes: knowledge acquisition (elicitation and, latterly, automatic extraction); knowledge representation (rules, frames, semantic networks, logics, description logics); knowledge modelling (CommonKADS model suites; problem-solving methods); ontology engineering (formal, explicit, shared conceptualisations); semantic interoperability (RDF/OWL and the Semantic Web stack); reusable methodologies and task/inference libraries; organisational and communication analysis; task, agent and communication models; knowledge-graph construction, integration and provenance; retrieval, agency and human–AI collaboration architectures; and — as Section 12 establishes — a substantial body of work on knowledge change, spanning truth maintenance, belief revision, ontology evolution, schema and temporal-data evolution, provenance and configuration management.

This inventory is deliberately extensive. A robust account of inherited capability is the necessary precondition for any credible claim of extension: no future methodology can be positioned as novel with respect to capabilities the field already possesses, including the capability to represent and manage change within a layer.

15. The Candidate Integrative Insight (the Candidate Gap)

Only after the preceding inventory — and especially after Section 12 — can the chapter responsibly state the candidate gap. It is not any of the following, each of which already exists: representing that knowledge changed (provenance); revising a belief set (AGM, truth maintenance); evolving an ontology (ontology-change research); versioning a schema or a model artefact (database and configuration management); or updating a graph.

The candidate gap is the cross-layer governance of knowledge change: an integrating methodology under which a proposed change is evidenced, challenged, authorised, propagated across the human, symbolic and parametric layers, evaluated for quality, and released as a new accepted state — with the decision rights and accountability that “governance” implies. Stated as an engineering object, this is the controlled transition of a knowledge system from one justified state to another.

The associated research questions — to be tested, not assumed, beyond the Landscape — include: continuous knowledge revision as a first-class operation; lifecycle governance across successive states; evidence-centred assertions carrying their justification; human–AI co-evolution with mutual correction; versioned “knowledge releases” with defined quality gates; knowledge quality as a function of time; and the notion of a “Knowledge Operating System” as the systemic locus of the above. Whether these constitute genuine gaps or under-recognised recombinations of existing work is the empirical question for the Literature Landscape.

16. Provisional Positioning of a Candidate Methodology

Consistent with the analysis above, the working position is deliberately restrained and is now stated in cross-layer terms.

A candidate methodology is best investigated not as the next historical stage of Knowledge Engineering, but as a cross-layer methodology for governed knowledge evolution. Its possible contribution would lie in integrating mature but separately developed capabilities — representation, modelling, ontology, provenance, quality control, organisational authority, retrieval and machine agency — around the controlled transition of a knowledge system from one justified state to another.

This formulation gives “knowledge evolution” an engineering meaning rather than leaving it as a metaphor, and it places a specific, falsifiable burden on any such methodology: to show that cross-layer governance of knowledge change is a coherent engineering object; that it is not already served, under another name, by an existing discipline or by a straightforward composition of the traditions catalogued in Sections 12 and 14; and that integrating those capabilities around it yields governance the parts do not deliver in isolation. Any candidate methodology should not be presented as a replacement for Knowledge Engineering, nor as its inevitable culmination, but as a synthesis whose value must be demonstrated against precisely the traditions most likely to have anticipated it.

Burden of Proof

Established (what is mature). The engineering primitives are individually mature: representation formalisms, ontology languages, large-scale graph integration, retrieval and agency architectures, and — per Section 12 — layer-specific mechanisms for knowledge change (truth maintenance, belief revision, ontology evolution, schema/temporal evolution, provenance, configuration management).

Unresolved (what remains open). What is not consolidated is a cross-layer methodology governing how a change is proposed, evidenced, challenged, authorised, propagated, evaluated and released across a Hybrid Symbolic–Parametric Knowledge System — the controlled transition from one justified state to another.

Implication (what the synthesis must explain). The Synthesis must establish whether this cross-layer governance is a coherent engineering object; whether it is already delivered under another name or by a straightforward composition of the catalogued traditions; and whether integrating the mature parts around it yields governance the parts do not provide alone. Whether any specific methodology discharges this burden is reserved for C01.

17. Relationship to the Position Paper

This document provides the historical and methodological evidence required for the Position Paper. It will support or revise claims concerning: the historical development of Knowledge Engineering; the expansion of its engineering object; the relationship between Knowledge Engineering and adjacent disciplines, including the knowledge-change traditions of Section 12; the maturity of existing methodologies; the scope of contemporary AI engineering; and the positioning of any candidate cross-layer governance methodology. That paper should draw only those conclusions the evidence supports, and in particular should not assert novelty for capabilities catalogued in Sections 12 and 14, nor present any methodology as a historical successor stage.

18. Guiding Principle

The organising principle of this document is intentionally simple:

The evolution of Knowledge Engineering is best understood through the changing object of engineering rather than through the chronology of technologies.

Applied consistently, that lens reveals both a genuine widening of scope across five decades and a specific, defensible candidate for what remains unconsolidated — not knowledge change, which the field has addressed repeatedly within layers, but the cross-layer governance of change across Hybrid Symbolic–Parametric Knowledge Systems.

Engineering alone does not explain how knowledge becomes enduring collective capability. That question motivates the next chapter, B03.
