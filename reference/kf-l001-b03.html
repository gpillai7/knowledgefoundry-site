KF-L001-B03 · Knowledge Beyond Engineering

How Knowledge Becomes Collective Capability

Evidence · Final · v1.0 · 37 min read

Question  →  Corpus  →  Evidence  →  Patterns  →  Candidate Insight

Purpose

To examine the major intellectual traditions that address how knowledge is created, shared, preserved, legitimised and sustained beyond the historical scope of Knowledge Engineering, and to evaluate what each contributes to the understanding of knowledge as enduring collective capability. The chapter writes out the eight traditions of Section 4 as analytical prose through an analytical lens of six recurring questions. Each tradition is allowed to conclude on its own terms; the explicit integration with the technical account of B02 is reserved for the chapter’s closing sections, in keeping with the Literature Landscape’s separation of evidence from theory.

Executive Summary

The previous chapter (B02) examined the historical evolution of Knowledge Engineering and argued that its trajectory is best understood as a progressive expansion of the object of engineering, ending in a contemporary convergence of symbolic and parametric systems. It concluded that the constituent capabilities for governed, continuously evolving knowledge systems are individually mature but not yet integrated, and located the candidate gap in the cross-layer governance of knowledge change — the controlled transition of a knowledge system from one justified state to another.

Knowledge Engineering, however, is only one of several intellectual traditions concerned with knowledge, and it is conspicuously the one least equipped to say what makes a knowledge state justified, who is authorised to change it, how it is preserved authentically over time, or how it becomes a durable capability of a group rather than a property of a machine. Parallel disciplines — Organisational Learning, Knowledge Management, Communities of Practice, Information Science, Library & Information Science, Archival Science, Social Epistemology and Knowledge Governance — developed largely independently and asked different questions. Rather than how knowledge should be represented or modelled, they asked how it is created, transferred, enacted, organised, preserved, legitimised and governed within organisations, institutions and societies.

This chapter examines these traditions through a shared analytical lens. Its purpose is not to compare disciplines descriptively but to identify the dimensions of knowledge that lie beyond the scope of Knowledge Engineering. Each tradition names its own object — learning, stewardship, participation, preservation, justification, governance — and this chapter’s interpretive contribution is to read them together as complementary accounts of how knowledge becomes enduring collective capability, a framing that belongs to the review rather than to the traditions themselves. Read this way, several of them supply precisely the elements that the technical traditions surveyed in B02 leave unaddressed: an account of adaptive revision (Organisational Learning), of tacit-to-explicit conversion (Knowledge Management), of practice-bound knowing that resists capture (Communities of Practice), of organisation and authentic preservation over time (Library & Information Science, Archival Science), of what makes a knowledge claim collectively justified (Social Epistemology), and of the authority and decision rights that govern knowledge behaviour (Knowledge Governance).

Consistent with the Literature Landscape methodology, whether the integration of these social and institutional accounts with B02’s technical account constitutes a genuinely distinct perspective, or a relabelling of existing ideas, is reserved for C01. As in B02, this chapter defines a burden of proof rather than asserting a conclusion.

Orientation for the Standalone Reader

This chapter follows B02 in the Literature Landscape and occasionally refers to it, but is intended to be intelligible on its own. In brief: B02 examined the engineering tradition that builds knowledge systems — spanning knowledge held explicitly in documents, rules, ontologies and graphs, and knowledge held implicitly in the parameters of machine-learning models — and concluded that no existing technical tradition fully addresses the governance of knowledge change across these different forms of representation: how a proposed change to what a system holds is evidenced, authorised and released, rather than merely recorded. B03 investigates whether the complementary, largely non-technical traditions surveyed here supply the social, institutional and epistemic foundations that those technical approaches leave out. Where terms such as “justified state” or “governed evolution” appear below, they carry that sense; a reader who has not read B02 loses none of this chapter’s argument.

1. Introduction

Knowledge Engineering represents only one tradition within a much broader intellectual landscape concerned with understanding knowledge. For all its sophistication, it addresses a particular question — how knowledge can be represented, modelled and made computable — and leaves largely untouched a wider set of questions about how knowledge is created, sustained, legitimised and governed by the human collectives that hold it. Those questions belong to other disciplines, and it is those disciplines that this chapter surveys.

The preceding chapter, B02, traced the engineering tradition through its changing object of engineering, showing how increasingly capable knowledge systems came to be built. It did not, and could not, explain how knowledge becomes an enduring organisational or institutional capability — how it is sustained, legitimised and governed by the people who create and depend on it. That is the question this chapter takes up.

During the same decades in which Knowledge Engineering evolved, several neighbouring disciplines approached knowledge from other directions. Some studied how organisations learn and adapt. Some studied how tacit expertise is cultivated and shared. Some studied how meaning is enacted through participation in a community. Others studied how recorded knowledge is organised, preserved and kept authentic across time, how communities determine what counts as knowledge, or how authority over knowledge is exercised. Taken together, these traditions broaden the understanding of knowledge well beyond its representation inside computational systems.

The purpose of this chapter is therefore not to revisit Knowledge Engineering but to investigate what these adjacent traditions contribute that Knowledge Engineering alone does not explain — and, specifically, whether they supply the human, social, institutional and temporal dimensions that B02 identified as the unmet half of governed knowledge evolution. A note on stance, carried over from B02: the chapter treats the “collective capability” framing as a hypothesis to be tested against the literature, not as a foregone conclusion, and it does not position any candidate methodology as the culmination of these traditions.

2. Central Research Question

The chapter is organised around one guiding question.

How does knowledge become enduring collective capability?

Supporting questions include: How is knowledge created? How is it shared? How is it preserved? How is it legitimised? How is it sustained over time? How do institutions learn? What roles do communities play? What roles do governance and stewardship play?

Read against B02, several of these map directly onto the components of “the controlled transition of a knowledge system from one justified state to another”: legitimised corresponds to justified; stewardship and governance to authorised and governed; preserved over time to the temporal dimension that B02’s technical traditions handled only partially.

3. Analytical Lens

Each tradition is examined through six recurring analytical questions — a flexible lens rather than a rigid scoring scheme. In practice, and as is normal in scholarly writing, some traditions naturally invite fuller treatment of their historical development and core contribution than of the other questions; the lens is used to structure comparison, not to force every tradition into equal coverage of every dimension.

4. Corpus of Investigation

Unlike B02, whose corpus consisted of historical developments within Knowledge Engineering, the corpus here consists of eight complementary traditions. Each is now examined in full through the analytical lens of Section 3.

4.1 Organisational Learning

Central question: How do organisations learn?

Development and key figures. Organisational Learning grew from the behavioural theory of the firm (Cyert & March 1963) into a distinct field through the action-science programme of Argyris and Schön (1978, 1996), Schön’s account of reflective practice (1983), and Senge’s popular synthesis of the “learning organisation” (1990). A parallel, more analytic current studied learning as the modification of organisational routines (Levitt & March 1988; Huber 1991), the balance between exploration and exploitation (March 1991), and the firm’s absorptive capacity for new knowledge (Cohen & Levinthal 1990).

Primary object. The learning process of a collective: how an organisation detects and corrects error, adapts to a changing environment, and encodes the results in routines. Knowledge here is a capacity for effective action, not an artefact.

Primary problem. Individual learning does not automatically become organisational learning; organisations develop defensive routines that block the correction of error; and environments change faster than tacit routines adapt.

Core contribution. The enduring contribution is Argyris and Schön’s distinction between single-loop learning — correcting action within existing governing assumptions — and double-loop learning — questioning and revising the governing assumptions themselves. Alongside it sit the learning-organisation disciplines (Senge 1990), the treatment of routines as organisational memory, and the exploration/exploitation tension (March 1991) that frames every learning system’s trade-off between refining what it knows and discovering what it does not.

Remaining limitations. The tradition is strong on process and adaptation but weak on representation, formalisation and machine-actionability; its notion of “organisational memory” is often metaphorical; and its constructs are notoriously hard to operationalise or measure.

Relationship to Knowledge Engineering. Complementary and largely non-overlapping. Where Knowledge Engineering builds explicit knowledge artefacts, Organisational Learning explains how a collective changes what it knows over time. Double-loop learning is, in effect, the human-organisational analogue of the belief-revision and ontology-change traditions catalogued in B02 (§12): both concern the disciplined revision not merely of content but of the governing criteria by which content is judged.

Relevance to governed knowledge evolution. Double-loop learning offers a precise model of revision that reaches the governing assumptions of a body of knowledge, not merely its content. Any continuously evolving knowledge system would need to inherit this adaptive capacity — and, with it, the tradition’s central caution: revising what counts as knowledge is a distinct and harder operation than revising individual claims, and a system that can only do the latter has not yet learned in the double-loop sense.

4.2 Knowledge Management

Central question: How is organisational knowledge cultivated?

Development and key figures. Knowledge Management drew its philosophical root from Polanyi’s account of tacit knowledge — “we can know more than we can tell” (Polanyi 1966) — and its defining theory from Nonaka’s dynamic model of knowledge creation (Nonaka 1994; Nonaka & Takeuchi 1995), later extended by the notion of Ba, a shared context for creation (Nonaka & Konno 1998). Davenport and Prusak (1998) supplied a pragmatic, organisational account of how knowledge is generated, codified and transferred, and Alavi and Leidner (2001) consolidated the study of knowledge-management systems. The field is closely tied to the knowledge-based view of the firm (Kogut & Zander 1992; Grant 1996).

Primary object. Organisational knowledge as a cultivable asset — and, centrally, the conversion between tacit and explicit knowledge.

Primary problem. Much of the knowledge on which organisations depend is tacit and person-bound; it is easily lost and rarely reused. How can it be cultivated, converted into shareable form, and transferred without being destroyed in the process?

Core contribution. The tacit/explicit distinction operationalised as a dynamic, through the SECI model — socialisation, externalisation, combination, internalisation — in which knowledge spirals between tacit and explicit modes across individuals and groups (Nonaka & Takeuchi 1995). With it came the reframing of knowledge as flow rather than only stock, the concept of Ba, and the practical distinction between codification and personalisation strategies.

Remaining limitations. The field has been criticised for over-optimism about the codifiability of tacit knowledge; many knowledge-management systems degenerated into document repositories; and the “management” framing tends to neglect legitimation and authority, treating knowledge as a resource to be moved rather than a claim to be warranted.

Relationship to Knowledge Engineering. The closest neighbour to Knowledge Engineering, and often its assumed complement: Knowledge Engineering supplied the technical substrate that Knowledge Management presupposed, while Knowledge Management supplied the human and social conversion processes that Knowledge Engineering black-boxed.

Relevance to governed knowledge evolution. SECI offers a tested account of how knowledge moves between tacit and explicit forms, and any governed knowledge system should inherit it as a model of knowledge in motion rather than at rest. It should equally inherit the field’s hard-won correction of its own early optimism: conversion between tacit expertise and explicit representation is lossy, effortful and frequently incomplete, and any system that treats codification as frictionless will capture the form of knowledge while losing much of its substance.

4.3 Communities of Practice

Central question: How does knowledge emerge through participation?

Development and key figures. The tradition originates in Lave and Wenger’s study of apprenticeship and situated learning (1991), was developed into a general social theory of learning by Wenger (1998), and was connected to organisational innovation by Brown and Duguid (1991, 2000).

Primary object. Knowledge as enacted through participation in a community of practice — situated, social and inseparable from identity and doing.

Primary problem. Learning is not the acquisition of abstract, decontextualised knowledge but a deepening participation in the practices of a community; newcomers become competent by moving from the periphery toward full membership. Much of what a community knows lives in its practice, not in its documents.

Core contribution. The concepts of legitimate peripheral participation and of the community of practice itself — defined by a shared domain, a community, and a practice (Wenger 1998). The tradition’s most consequential claim is that knowing is inseparable from practice and identity, and that a great deal of expertise cannot be extracted into any representation without remainder. Brown and Duguid added the mechanisms of brokering and boundary objects by which knowledge moves between communities.

Remaining limitations. The tradition resists codification by design, which makes it hard to govern, scale or deliberately manage; it can romanticise community and cohesion; and it says little about artefacts, preservation or formal systems.

Relationship to Knowledge Engineering. In large part a critique of Knowledge Engineering’s founding assumption — that expert knowledge can be elicited and represented. It complements the engineering tradition by locating the tacit, practice-bound knowing that the “knowledge acquisition bottleneck” of B02 (§6) repeatedly ran aground on.

Relevance to governed knowledge evolution. Communities of Practice stand as a standing caution: a substantial part of expertise is practice-bound and cannot be extracted into any representation without remainder. Any knowledge system should therefore expect to connect to living practice rather than assume it can capture it — and the tradition’s concepts of brokering and boundary objects offer a tested model for translating knowledge across the boundaries between communities, which is a more realistic ambition than extraction.

4.4 Information Science

Central question: What is information?

Development and key figures. Information Science, in the sense relevant here, is the foundational inquiry into the nature of information itself, distinct from Shannon’s mathematical theory of communication (Shannon 1948), which addressed signal transmission rather than meaning. Its conceptual anchors are Buckland’s analysis of the meanings of “information” (Buckland 1991) and Bates’s account of the discipline’s implicit foundations and of information as a documentary substrate (Bates 1999, 2006).

Primary object. The nature of information as such, and the processes by which it is represented, organised, retrieved and used.

Primary problem. To give a coherent account of “information” that spans documents, meaning and signals, and to study — as Bates puts it — the documentary products of every other discipline, making Information Science a kind of meta-science.

Core contribution. Buckland’s tripartite distinction between information-as-process, information-as-knowledge and information-as-thing — together with the observation that whatever a storage-and-retrieval system holds is necessarily information-as-thing — an object standing in for knowing, not knowing itself. Bates’s work traces the physical, documentary substrate that underlies the field’s more visible concerns.

Remaining limitations. The tradition is foundational and definitional rather than prescriptive; it clarifies what information is without specifying how organisational capability is built or how knowledge is governed.

Relationship to Knowledge Engineering. It provides a conceptual hygiene that Knowledge Engineering often lacks — a disciplined separation between the thing stored and the knowing it is meant to support.

Relevance to governed knowledge evolution. This distinction yields what may be the single most important clarification the chapter can offer a designer of knowledge systems: every knowledge system governs representations, not knowledge itself. What such a system stores, versions, transmits and revises are things that stand in for knowing; whether they warrant the knowing attributed to them is a separate question, and it is the question on which validation, quality and trust ultimately turn. Information Science reached this conclusion on its own terms — Bates characterises the discipline’s core activity as the representation and organisation of information rather than the knowing of it — and a system that mistakes its representations for the knowledge they represent will govern the operational knowledge object while neglecting the thing it is meant to serve.

4.5 Library & Information Science

Central question: How should knowledge be organised and preserved?

Development and key figures. Library & Information Science furnishes the long tradition of organising recorded knowledge for access. Its landmarks include Otlet’s vision of universal documentation and a networked knowledge system (Otlet 1934), which anticipated hypertext and the web; Ranganathan’s foundational classification theory and his Five Laws of Library Science (Ranganathan 1931); and Buckland’s later bridging of library thought and information science.

Primary object. The organisation, description, classification and preservation of recorded knowledge so that it can be found and used.

Primary problem. How to bring the growing mass of recorded knowledge under bibliographic control so that a reader can find what they need and the collection remains navigable as it grows.

Core contribution. A mature theory of classification and description — most influentially Ranganathan’s faceted (colon) classification — and the Five Laws (books are for use; every reader their book; every book its reader; save the time of the reader; the library is a growing organism). Otlet’s parallel contribution was the vision of a universal, interlinked repository of knowledge decades before the technology existed to build it.

Remaining limitations. The tradition is historically artefact- and document-centred; it tends to assume relatively stable objects and says less about tacit knowing or about continuous, machine-actionable evolution.

Relationship to Knowledge Engineering. Library & Information Science is the discipline of organising knowledge for human access; ontology engineering (B02 §9) inherited much of its classification theory and can be read as its machine-inferable descendant.

Relevance to governed knowledge evolution. Two inheritances stand out. First, the mature apparatus of classification, description and metadata for organising a body of knowledge so that it remains navigable as it grows. Second, Ranganathan’s fifth law — the library is a growing organism — which names, a century early, a design requirement any modern knowledge collection must meet: to be built to change continuously rather than to sit still.

4.6 Archival Science

Central question: How should institutional memory endure?

Development and key figures. Archival Science supplies the theory of preserving records and institutional memory over time. Its classical poles are Jenkinson’s custodial theory, with its emphasis on the impartiality and authenticity of records and the sanctity of evidence (Jenkinson 1922), and Schellenberg’s theory of appraisal and of the primary and secondary values of records (Schellenberg 1956). The late-twentieth-century “postcustodial” turn reconceived the field around the records continuum (Upward 1996, with McKemmish) and around shifting paradigms of evidence, memory and community (Cook 2013); digital preservation added reference models for maintaining authenticity over time (the OAIS reference model, CCSDS 2012 / ISO 14721).

Primary object. Records and institutional memory: how evidence of activity is preserved authentically, with its context intact, and made accessible across time and changes of custody.

Primary problem. How to preserve the authenticity, integrity and context of records as they age and pass through successive custodians — and how to decide, through appraisal, what should be kept at all.

Core contribution. The principles of provenance and respect des fonds (records are grouped and understood by their origin), original order, and the record as evidence; appraisal theory, which weighs the values of records over time; and the records continuum, which reconceives recordkeeping not as a linear lifecycle but as a set of simultaneous dimensions — creating, capturing, organising and pluralising — in which change is constitutive rather than incidental (Upward 1996).

Remaining limitations. The tradition is preservation- and evidence-centred rather than creation- or inference-centred; appraisal is inescapably value-laden and contested; and its classical form assumed a custodial stability that digital records disrupt.

Relationship to Knowledge Engineering. Archival provenance is the human and institutional ancestor of the machine provenance formalised in B02 (§10, §12): the W3C PROV data model can be read as a computational descendant of respect des fonds and chain of custody. The record-as-evidence maps onto the idea of evidence-centred knowledge, and the tradition directly complements Knowledge Engineering’s weak treatment of time and authenticity.

Relevance to governed knowledge evolution. Archival Science is the tradition most directly concerned with keeping knowledge trustworthy as it changes hands and ages. Its apparatus — provenance, appraisal (deciding what to promote and what to retain), authenticity, and the records continuum’s treatment of change as constitutive rather than incidental — is precisely what any system managing an evolving body of knowledge must inherit. The demanding extension it implies is to carry that apparatus from records to hybrid symbolic–parametric knowledge, a setting in which authenticity and chain of custody are far harder to establish for a model weight than for a document.

4.7 Social Epistemology

Central question: How does a community determine what counts as knowledge?

Development and key figures. Social Epistemology is the philosophical study of the social dimensions of knowing. Its two most influential programmes are Goldman’s veritistic social epistemology, which evaluates social practices by their tendency to produce true belief (Goldman 1999), and Fuller’s more constructivist, policy-oriented programme, which treats the organisation of knowledge production as a normative and institutional problem (Fuller 1988). Adjacent to both is the epistemology of testimony and trust — the recognition that most of what anyone knows rests on the word of others (Hardwig 1991).

Primary object. How communities and social practices produce, warrant and distribute knowledge — in short, collective justification.

Primary problem. Almost everything anyone knows depends on others: on testimony, on expertise, on institutions. How do social arrangements confer or undermine epistemic warrant, and how should a non-expert rationally assess competing experts?

Core contribution. A framework for the veritistic evaluation of knowledge-producing practices; an epistemology of testimony and trust; an account of the division of cognitive labour; and analyses of expertise, consensus and dissent as epistemic mechanisms. Collectively these give a principled account of what it means for a claim to be warranted for a community rather than for an isolated knower.

Remaining limitations. The tradition is largely philosophical and normative rather than operational; it is internally contested (veritistic versus constructivist camps); and it specifies criteria of good epistemic practice without specifying the mechanisms or systems that would enact them.

Relationship to Knowledge Engineering. It supplies the normative account of justification that Knowledge Engineering’s “knowledge base” silently presupposes but never theorises: what makes an asserted fact warranted, and for whom.

Relevance to governed knowledge evolution. Social Epistemology supplies what engineering accounts assume but never theorise: an account of what makes a claim warranted for a community rather than merely asserted. Any system that purports to hold or revise a body of accepted knowledge takes an implicit position on collective justification — on who may warrant a claim, on what evidence, and for whom. Any such system would need to make that position explicit and, harder still, operational, turning philosophical criteria of good epistemic practice into mechanisms a system can actually apply.

4.8 Knowledge Governance

Central question: Who has the authority to shape organisational knowledge?

Development and key figures. The knowledge-governance approach emerged from organisational economics and strategy. The term was introduced by Grandori (1997) and developed into a micro-level theory of governance mechanisms and the theory of the firm (Grandori 2001); Foss characterised it as a distinct, emerging approach cutting across knowledge management, organisation studies and strategy (Foss 2007), later consolidated with Michailova (Foss & Michailova 2009). It is closely linked to the knowledge-based view of the firm (Kogut & Zander 1992; Grant 1996).

Primary object. The deliberate design of mechanisms — structures, incentives, rules, decision rights and authority — that influence knowledge processes such as sharing, creation, transfer and retention.

Primary problem. Knowledge processes do not self-organise optimally. How should an organisation deploy governance mechanisms, at the micro level of individual behaviour, to shape how knowledge is created, shared and retained?

Core contribution. The reframing of knowledge management as a governance problem: the recognition that a portfolio of mechanisms (hierarchy, market, hybrid forms, incentives, allocations of decision rights) shapes knowledge behaviour, and that authority and decision rights over knowledge are first-class design variables rather than background conditions.

Remaining limitations. The tradition is firm- and economics-centric; it is concerned with influencing human behaviour rather than with the technical governance of knowledge artefacts or models; and it says comparatively little about preservation or authenticity.

Relationship to Knowledge Engineering. It is the governance complement that Knowledge Engineering lacks: where the engineering tradition builds systems, the governance tradition asks who decides, under what authority, and through which mechanisms behaviour is shaped.

Relevance to governed knowledge evolution. Knowledge Governance supplies the vocabulary of authority and decision rights — who may change what, through which mechanism, with what legitimacy — that technical accounts of knowledge systems characteristically lack. Any such methodology would need to adapt this account from the governance of human knowledge behaviour, which the original theory addressed, to the governance of changes in an engineered knowledge base, which it did not anticipate.

Recurring themes. Across the traditions, knowledge is treated as social and collective rather than individual; as substantially tacit and context-bound; as enacted in practice and preserved in records; as requiring legitimation to count as knowledge for a community; and as subject to governance and stewardship. No single tradition holds all of these, but every theme recurs across several.

Complementary perspectives. The traditions divide the labour of explanation. Organisational Learning explains adaptive change; Knowledge Management explains creation and tacit-to-explicit conversion; Communities of Practice explain situated, practice-bound knowing; Information Science clarifies what information is; Library & Information Science organises and describes recorded knowledge; Archival Science preserves it authentically over time; Social Epistemology accounts for collective justification; and Knowledge Governance specifies authority and mechanisms. Read together, they populate the human, social, institutional and temporal space that Knowledge Engineering largely left empty.

Conceptual overlaps. The boundaries are porous. Organisational Learning, Knowledge Management and Communities of Practice overlap heavily on tacit knowledge and its transfer. Library & Information Science and Archival Science share the concerns of organisation, description and preservation. Social Epistemology and Knowledge Governance both address legitimation and authority — the former philosophically, the latter as organisational design. These overlaps are not redundancy but triangulation: the same phenomenon seen from different disciplinary vantage points.

Persistent blind spots. The mirror image of Knowledge Engineering’s blind spot appears here. With the partial exception of Information Science and the digital-preservation strand of Archival Science, these traditions under-theorise the machine and technical layer and say little about continuous, machine-actionable evolution. Where B02’s traditions could represent and compute knowledge but not govern its human legitimacy, B03’s traditions can theorise legitimacy, learning and preservation but not render them machine-actionable. Neither side, alone, spans the gap.

Mature capabilities. Several capabilities are, across these traditions, genuinely mature: organising and describing recorded knowledge (Library & Information Science); preserving it authentically with provenance and appraisal (Archival Science); modelling knowledge creation and tacit-explicit conversion (Knowledge Management); explaining situated knowing and its limits (Communities of Practice); accounting for collective justification (Social Epistemology); designing governance mechanisms and decision rights (Knowledge Governance); and explaining adaptive, double-loop learning (Organisational Learning). This maturity is the precondition, as in B02, for stating precisely what is not yet integrated.

6. Cross-Tradition Patterns

The chapter’s scaffold proposed a candidate progression in which the principal object expands from the individual toward larger social systems: individual → team → organisation → community → institution → society. Evaluated against the traditions, this progression captures something real but is too simple to stand as stated.

It is real in that the traditions do collectively widen the knowing subject well beyond the individual, and that the larger social units (institution, society) are precisely the ones Knowledge Engineering never addressed. It is too simple in that the traditions do not line up along a single scale. Communities of Practice and Archival Science both concern collectives, but along quite different dimensions — participation versus preservation — and neither is simply “larger” than the other.

A more defensible reading, offered here as a provisional pattern supported by the present review rather than a validated finding, is that the traditions jointly populate three complementary dimensions rather than one ladder:

  • Scale — the knowing subject, from individual to society;
  • Capability — the knowledge activity in question: creation, sharing, enactment, organisation, preservation, legitimation and governance;
  • Time — the temporal horizon, from momentary learning to enduring institutional memory.

Named this way — Scale → Capability → Time — these three dimensions may serve as recurring analytical lenses across the wider Literature Landscape, not only within this chapter: each subsequent tradition or body of evidence can be located by where it sits on the three, which offers a consistent basis for comparison across chapters.

On this reading the traditions do not form a succession in which each supersedes the last, any more than the engineering periods of B02 did. They form a division of intellectual labour: an accretion of complementary capabilities across the three dimensions, with no single tradition covering all of them. The apparent “progression from the individual to society” is better understood as the field, taken as a whole, having gradually populated the Scale dimension while separately populating those of Capability and Time.

6.1 Limitations of This Review

As with B02, this chapter is a work of interpretive synthesis and its conclusions should be read with four limitations in mind.

First, it is a selective narrative synthesis rather than a systematic review: the eight traditions and their representative authors were chosen for canonical standing and bearing on the guiding question, not assembled through an exhaustive, protocol-driven search; the absence of a tradition or source here is not evidence of its absence from the field.

Second, it is organised around a single interpretive lens — knowledge as enduring collective capability, read as a complement to the technical account of B02. A different organising principle (for example, the economics of knowledge, science and technology studies, or the philosophy of science) would foreground different traditions and yield a different, equally legitimate synthesis.

Third, it is weighted toward Western, English-language scholarship and toward the management, information and philosophical literatures. It under-represents non-Western epistemologies, indigenous and oral knowledge traditions, and relevant work in sociology, anthropology and science and technology studies that treats several of these problems under other names.

Fourth, it is not a complete treatment of any of the eight traditions, each of which sustains its own internal debates (for example, the veritistic–constructivist division in Social Epistemology, or the custodial–postcustodial division in Archival Science) that are only gestured at here. The Position Paper and the Literature Synthesis will need to engage the governance, legitimation and preservation literatures more fully than an evidence chapter can.

These limitations bound the chapter’s claims without undermining its argument: what is offered is a defensible reading of the adjacent traditions and of what they contribute to governed knowledge evolution, not a settled or exhaustive account.

7. Existing Contributions

Across these traditions, several capabilities can reasonably be regarded as mature bodies of scholarship rather than emerging ideas. They are set out plainly here, so that no unsupported claim of novelty can later be made on their behalf:

  • organisational learning — single- and double-loop learning, learning organisations, routines as memory, exploration/exploitation (Organisational Learning);
  • knowledge creation — the tacit/explicit distinction, the SECI conversion spiral, Ba as a context for creation (Knowledge Management);
  • situated knowing — legitimate peripheral participation, communities of practice, brokering and boundary objects (Communities of Practice);
  • conceptual foundations of information — the information-as-thing/-as-process/-as-knowledge distinction (Information Science);
  • organisation and description — classification theory, faceted classification, metadata, the collection as a growing organism (Library & Information Science);
  • institutional memory and stewardship — provenance, original order, appraisal, authenticity over time, the records continuum, digital-preservation reference models (Archival Science);
  • collective justification — veritistic evaluation, the epistemology of testimony and trust, the division of cognitive labour (Social Epistemology);
  • governance — governance mechanisms, decision rights and authority over knowledge processes (Knowledge Governance).

This inventory, together with B02’s inventory of engineering capabilities, defines the full stock of inherited capability against which any claim of novelty for a future methodology must be tested.

8. Candidate Integrative Insight

Only after analysing the traditions can the chapter state its candidate integrative observation — and, as in B02, it is stated with restraint, and as an interpretation advanced by the review rather than a claim drawn from the literature.

The traditions surveyed here do not describe themselves as accounts of “collective capability.” Each names its own object: learning, creation, participation, organisation, preservation, justification, governance. The interpretive move this chapter makes — and it is the chapter’s own contribution, not a finding belonging to any single tradition — is to read these objects together as complementary answers to one question: how knowledge becomes an enduring capability of a collective rather than a possession of an individual or a property of a machine.

Read this way, a pattern emerges that is worth stating carefully. The dimensions of knowledge these traditions theorise are, to a striking degree, the dimensions that a purely technical account of knowledge systems leaves untouched. Organisational Learning theorises adaptive, double-loop revision; Knowledge Management the movement between tacit and explicit; Communities of Practice the practice-bound knowing that resists capture; Library & Information Science and Archival Science the organisation, preservation and authentication of knowledge over time; Social Epistemology what makes a claim collectively justified; and Knowledge Governance the authority and decision rights by which a change becomes legitimate. Where the engineering tradition can represent, compute and version knowledge, these traditions can say what makes a body of knowledge trustworthy, legitimate and durable for the people who depend on it.

The candidate integrative insight is therefore not that “collective capability” is a rival framing to engineering, nor that any single tradition here completes what engineering began. It is the more modest observation that the technical and the social-institutional accounts of knowledge appear to be complementary rather than competing, and that each names capabilities the other lacks. Consistent with the Literature Landscape methodology, whether their integration constitutes a genuinely distinct contribution — and where, precisely, the overall gap lies — is reserved for C01; this chapter’s task is the narrower evidential one of establishing what the adjacent traditions contribute and what they leave out.

9. Burden of Proof

Consistent with the Literature Landscape methodology, this chapter defines evidence, not theory. It sets out the portion of the evidential burden — arising from the traditions surveyed here — that any future methodology would have to satisfy, structured around the three standard questions.

Established (what is mature). The eight traditions supply genuinely mature capabilities: double-loop organisational learning; the SECI account of tacit–explicit conversion; situated, practice-bound knowing and its limits; the conceptual foundations of information; classification, description and metadata; provenance, appraisal and authenticity over time; collective justification; and governance mechanisms and decision rights.

Unresolved (what remains open). These traditions theorise legitimacy, learning and preservation but do not render them machine-actionable. On the evidence of this chapter, a methodology claiming to govern knowledge as an enduring collective capability would still need to show that it:

  • accommodates adaptive, double-loop revision — the revision of governing assumptions, not merely of content (Organisational Learning);
  • treats the conversion between tacit and explicit knowledge as lossy and effortful rather than frictionless (Knowledge Management);
  • connects to practice-bound knowing rather than assuming it can be captured (Communities of Practice);
  • rests on a defensible account of the difference between the thing stored and the knowing it supports (Information Science);
  • inherits the mature apparatus of organisation, description and preservation, including the expectation that a collection must be built to grow (Library & Information Science);
  • carries provenance, appraisal and authenticity forward from records to hybrid symbolic–parametric knowledge (Archival Science);
  • makes explicit and operational an account of collective justification — what warrants a change, and for whom (Social Epistemology); and
  • specifies authority and decision rights over knowledge change, adapted from human behaviour to an engineered knowledge base (Knowledge Governance).

Implication (what the synthesis must explain). The synthesis must explain how these social-institutional accounts integrate with B02’s technical account — whether meeting these requirements together is a genuinely distinct contribution or a recombination of mature parts. This chapter asserts neither; it leaves the verdict to the Position Paper and to C01.

10. Relationship to Previous and Subsequent Chapters

B03 is the social-institutional complement to B02’s technical history: B02 asked how the object of Knowledge Engineering evolved, while B03 asks how knowledge becomes enduring collective capability. Together they supply two complementary bodies of evidence — engineering knowledge systems, and sustaining knowledge within organisations and institutions — neither sufficient alone. The remaining evidence chapters extend the survey to further traditions, and it is the Literature Synthesis, rather than any single chapter, that weighs the combined evidence and identifies the overall gap, if any.

11. Guiding Principle

The organising principle of this chapter is intentionally simple.

Knowledge Engineering explains how knowledge systems are engineered. Adjacent traditions explain how knowledge becomes enduring collective capability.

These are complementary explanations of a single broader phenomenon. The questions Knowledge Engineering cannot answer — what makes a knowledge state justified, who may authorise its change, how it endures authentically over time — are precisely the questions these adjacent traditions were built to answer.

Capability alone does not explain how legitimate change occurs. That becomes the focus of B04.
